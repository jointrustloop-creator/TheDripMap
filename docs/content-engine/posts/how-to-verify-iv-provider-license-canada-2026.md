Before an IV goes into your arm, one fact matters more than any review or photo: whether the person doing it, and the person who prescribed what is in the bag, are actually licensed to. In Canada you do not have to take a clinic's word for it. Every regulated health professional in the country appears on a public register you can search for free, in about two minutes, from your phone.

This guide shows you exactly how, province by province. It is a practical how-to, not legal or medical advice. For the rules themselves, who is allowed to do what, see our companion guide on [who can legally give you an IV across Canada](/blog/who-can-legally-give-iv-canada-rules-by-province-2026). For how we check clinics on this site, see [how we verify clinics](/verification).

## The three things worth checking

Most people think of this as one question. It is really three, and clinics differ on all of them.

1. **Who administers the IV.** The person inserting the line and running the infusion. Usually a registered nurse, sometimes a nurse practitioner, physician, or an authorized naturopathic doctor.
2. **Who prescribes it.** Someone with prescribing authority has to stand behind what is in the bag. A registered nurse can administer an IV, but an RN alone is not a prescriber. In practice that means a physician, a nurse practitioner, or, in Ontario, a naturopathic doctor holding the province's specific IV authorization.
3. **Whether the premises is authorized.** This one surprises people. Depending on the province and who runs the clinic, the physical location may itself need to be registered and inspected, with the result published.

A clinic that answers all three clearly is telling you something real. A clinic that will not name anyone is telling you something too.

## Ontario

Ontario has the most complete public paper trail in the country, including something no other province publishes for IV clinics: inspection outcomes for the premises itself.

**Physicians.** Search the [College of Physicians and Surgeons of Ontario register](https://register.cpso.on.ca/) by name. The entry shows registration status, class of certificate, and any restrictions, plus discipline history where it exists.

**Nurses and nurse practitioners.** Search [Find a Nurse at the College of Nurses of Ontario](https://registry.cno.org/). You can look up an RN, RPN, or NP by name and see registration status and any restrictions on their practice.

**Naturopathic doctors.** Search the [College of Naturopaths of Ontario public register](https://cono.alinityapp.com/client/publicdirectory). Here the detail matters. Being a registered ND is not enough to run an IV in Ontario. Look at the entry's extended services: an ND who can legally prescribe and administer intravenous therapy will show both therapeutic prescribing and intravenous infusion therapy authorization. If those are absent, that ND cannot legally give you an IV, whatever the clinic's website says.

**The premises, for ND-run clinics.** Ontario requires every location where a naturopathic doctor performs intravenous infusion therapy to be registered and inspected by the College, and it publishes the result on the [IVIT premises register](https://cono.alinityapp.com/client/findcorporationdirectory). You can search by clinic name and city. The register shows whether the premises is authorized, what it is authorized to do (compounding, administering, or both), the inspection outcome, and any restrictions.

That last register is worth knowing about even if you never use it, because it tells you what a serious standard looks like: physical environment, infection control, sterile compounding, emergency preparedness, and record keeping, all checked by the regulator on a repeating cycle.

One asymmetry to be aware of. If a nurse gives you the same drip in a clinic operating under a physician's orders rather than an ND's, there is no equivalent premises inspection program for that setting in Ontario. The practitioners are regulated; the facility is not inspected the same way. That is not a reason to avoid nurse-run clinics, which are the majority and are staffed by regulated professionals. It is a reason to ask who prescribes and to check that person on the CPSO or CNO register yourself.

## British Columbia

**Nurses.** Search the [BC College of Nurses and Midwives register](https://registry.bccnm.ca/) by name for registration status and practice limits. BCCNM also publishes public notices and discipline outcomes on its website.

**Naturopathic doctors.** Since 2024, BC naturopaths are regulated by the College of Complementary Health Professionals of BC. Use the [CCHPBC practitioner search](https://cchpbc.ca/public/practitioner-search/). As in Ontario, a BC naturopath needs additional college certification to administer IV therapy, so check the certifications shown on the entry rather than assuming.

**Physicians.** The College of Physicians and Surgeons of BC publishes a public physician directory on its website, searchable by name.

BC does not publish an IV premises inspection register equivalent to Ontario's. In BC the practitioner registers are your main tool.

## Alberta

**Physicians.** The College of Physicians and Surgeons of Alberta publishes a physician search on [cpsa.ca](https://cpsa.ca/). Worth knowing: in 2026 CPSA published guidance discouraging arrangements where a physician is named as a clinic's medical director without genuinely prescribing for individual patients. If an Alberta clinic names a medical director, it is fair to ask whether that physician actually assesses and prescribes for patients rather than signing off on clinic stock.

**Nurses.** The College of Registered Nurses of Alberta publishes a public register on its website, searchable by name.

**Naturopathic doctors.** Search the [College of Naturopathic Doctors of Alberta public register](https://www.cnda.net/public-register-1).

## Quebec

Quebec works differently, and the difference matters. Naturopaths are not a regulated health profession in Quebec, which means they cannot legally administer intravenous therapy there at all. Anyone offering you an IV in Quebec should be a nurse or a physician.

**Nurses.** The Ordre des infirmières et infirmiers du Québec publishes a public roll of members on its website.

**Physicians.** The Collège des médecins du Québec publishes a searchable list of licensed physicians.

If a Quebec clinic tells you a naturopath will administer your IV, that is not a paperwork gap. It is outside what the profession is permitted to do in that province. Our [Quebec guide](/blog/who-can-legally-give-iv-quebec-2026) explains why.

## Everywhere else

The pattern holds across the rest of the country. Every province regulates nurses and physicians through a college, and each college publishes a public register on its website. Search the college name plus the words public register and you will find it. Naturopathic regulation varies by province: some regulate the profession and issue a separate IV authorization, others do not regulate it at all.

## What a good entry looks like

When you find the person, read the entry rather than just confirming a name appears.

- **Status is current.** Look for active, practising, or the province's equivalent. Expired, inactive, suspended, or resigned all mean the person is not currently entitled to practise.
- **The name actually matches.** Common names produce several results. Check the city and the registration number the clinic gave you, not just the spelling.
- **Restrictions are listed and readable.** Colleges publish limits, conditions, and undertakings on the entry. A restriction is not automatically alarming, but you are entitled to know it is there.
- **For naturopaths, the IV authorization is explicitly shown.** Registered is not the same as authorized to give IVs.
- **For Ontario ND clinics, the premises appears on the IVIT register with a pass outcome.**

If a clinic gives you a registration number that does not appear, or that belongs to someone else, that is worth walking away over.

## Reasonable questions to ask, before you look anything up

You do not need to arrive as an investigator. These are ordinary questions a well-run clinic answers without friction.

- Who will be starting my IV, and what is their credential?
- Who prescribes what goes in the bag, and are they registered in this province?
- Can I have the registration number of the prescriber?
- Where are your IV bags compounded or sourced from?
- What happens if I react badly during the infusion?

A clinic that treats those as intrusive has answered you anyway.

## If something is wrong

If the register contradicts what a clinic told you, the college is the place to raise it. Every college listed above runs a complaints process, linked from its website, and complaints about unauthorized practice are exactly what those processes exist for. Ontario's College of Naturopaths, for example, publicly asked patients to verify authorization and premises registration after it saw a run of naturopaths providing IV therapy without the required qualifications.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I check if a nurse is licensed in Canada?

Search the nursing college in that province, then look up the nurse by name on its public register. In Ontario it is Find a Nurse at the College of Nurses of Ontario; in British Columbia it is the BC College of Nurses and Midwives register. The entry shows whether registration is current and whether any restrictions apply. It is free and takes about a minute.

### Can any naturopath give IV therapy in Canada?

No. It depends on the province, and on the individual. In Ontario a naturopathic doctor needs specific intravenous infusion therapy authorization from the College of Naturopaths of Ontario, shown on their public register entry, and must work from premises the College has registered and inspected. In British Columbia additional college certification is required. In Quebec naturopaths are not a regulated health profession and cannot administer IV therapy at all.

### What is the IVIT premises register?

It is the College of Naturopaths of Ontario's public list of locations authorized to perform intravenous infusion therapy. Ontario requires each such premises to be registered and inspected, covering things like sterile compounding, infection control, and emergency preparedness, and the College publishes the outcome. You can search it by clinic name and city.

### Does a licensed clinic mean the treatment is safe or effective?

No, and it is important to keep the two apart. A register tells you a person is entitled to practise and is accountable to a regulator. It says nothing about whether a particular drip is appropriate for you, or whether the claims made about it are supported. Whether IV therapy makes sense for your situation is a conversation for you and a clinician.

### What if a clinic will not give me a name or registration number?

You are entitled to ask, and there is no confidentiality reason a clinic cannot tell you who prescribes and who administers. If a clinic declines, treat that as information. There are clinics that will answer plainly.
