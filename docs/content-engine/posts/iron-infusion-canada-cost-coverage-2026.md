Iron infusion is the odd one out on this site. Almost everything else under the IV umbrella is wellness: optional, elective, and mostly aimed at people who feel fine. An iron infusion is a medical treatment for a diagnosed deficiency, prescribed because bloodwork showed a problem. If you landed here after a doctor said your ferritin was low, you are in a different situation from someone booking a vitamin drip, and the money question works differently too.

This guide covers what an iron infusion costs in Canada, what public health insurance does and does not pay for, and why people with a legitimate prescription still end up paying privately. It is general information, not medical or financial advice, and coverage rules change. Confirm the specifics with your own doctor and your provincial plan.

## First, the honest caveat about sources

There is a reason this question is hard to research. Almost every page ranking for iron infusion cost in Canada is published by a clinic that sells iron infusions. That does not make their numbers wrong, and we cite them below, but it does mean nobody in the results has an incentive to tell you that waiting for the covered option might be fine for you. Treat the figures as reported prices from commercial sources, and confirm your own coverage with your hospital or plan directly.

## The two paths

For most Canadians there are two routes to the same treatment, and they differ enormously in cost and speed.

**The hospital route.** You get a referral, join a queue, and receive the infusion in a hospital outpatient clinic. In Ontario, [Care& reports](https://careand.ca/blog/iron-infusion-cost-ontario-ohip-coverage) that this route typically costs the patient nothing, with the infusion covered and the iron itself supplied through the hospital. The catch is time: the same source describes non-urgent waits in Toronto stretching from several weeks to several months.

**The private clinic route.** You pay, and you are usually seen quickly. Care& puts most private Toronto clinics at **$400 to $900 per session**. Other clinic sources quote the medication separately, with single-dose products in the several-hundred-dollar range and older multi-session products costing less per session but requiring more of them.

The pattern people run into is straightforward. The covered option is slow, the fast option is not covered, and the gap between them is where the frustration lives. Anyone who has sat with a ferritin result in the single digits while waiting months for an appointment understands the appeal of paying.

## What is actually covered, and where sources disagree

Here the clinic sources we found do not fully agree, which is itself worth knowing.

Both describe the same broad structure: provincial health insurance covers the treatment in a **hospital** setting, and generally does not cover an infusion given at a **standalone private clinic**, even when a physician ordered it.

Where they diverge is the drug. One source describes the iron medication as covered along with the infusion in the hospital setting; another distinguishes between the administration, which public insurance pays for, and the medication, which it says is typically supplied through the hospital's own formulary rather than by the provincial plan. For a patient in a hospital chair these usually amount to the same experience of paying nothing, but they are not the same mechanism, and they can produce different answers at different hospitals.

The practical takeaway: ask the hospital or clinic directly whether you will be billed for the drug, the administration, or both, and get the answer before your appointment rather than after.

Private extended health plans are a separate question again. Some reimburse part of a private infusion; many do not. Our guide on [how to claim IV therapy on insurance in Canada](/blog/how-to-claim-iv-therapy-insurance-canada-2026) covers how to ask your insurer properly.

## Before cost, the question that comes first

An iron infusion should follow evidence that you need one. That normally means bloodwork, typically ferritin along with a broader iron panel, and a clinician deciding that oral iron is not the right answer for you, whether because it has not worked, you cannot tolerate it, or the deficiency needs correcting faster than tablets can manage.

This is the line that separates an iron infusion from a wellness drip. If a clinic offers you an iron infusion without recent bloodwork establishing deficiency, that is a reason to stop and ask why. It is also the single clearest example of the wider point we make in [who should not get IV therapy](/blog/who-should-not-get-iv-therapy-canada-2026): the treatment is not the problem, the absence of assessment is.

## Not all iron products are the same

You may be quoted for different products, and the difference is mostly about how many visits you need.

- **Single-dose products** such as ferric derisomaltose or ferric carboxymaltose can often deliver a full replacement dose in one visit. They cost more per dose.
- **Older multi-session products** such as iron sucrose cost less per session, but a course typically runs several sessions.

When you compare private clinic quotes, compare the **total course**, not the per-session price. One $800 visit can be cheaper than six visits at $200, and it is also five fewer afternoons of your life.

## Questions worth asking

- Does my bloodwork actually show iron deficiency, and can I see the numbers?
- Is oral iron a reasonable option for me first?
- Can I be referred to a hospital outpatient clinic, and what is the realistic wait?
- If I go privately, what is the total cost for the full course, including the drug and the administration?
- Which iron product, and how many sessions?
- Will I be monitored during the infusion, and by whom?

That last question matters. Iron infusions are given by regulated professionals, and you can [look up who is administering and prescribing yours on a public register](/blog/how-to-verify-iv-provider-license-canada-2026).

## Frequently asked questions

### Is an iron infusion covered by OHIP?

The structure reported by Ontario clinic sources is that infusions given in a hospital outpatient setting are covered, while infusions at standalone private clinics generally are not, even with a physician's order. Sources differ on exactly how the iron medication itself is paid for in hospital, so confirm with the specific hospital or clinic whether you will be billed for the drug, the administration, or neither.

### How much does an iron infusion cost privately in Canada?

Care& reports most private Toronto clinics charging $400 to $900 per session, with the product affecting the total: single-dose products cost more per visit but may need only one, while older products cost less per session across a longer course. Prices outside Toronto vary. Always ask for the total cost of the full course rather than a per-session figure.

### Why would I pay privately if hospitals cover it?

Time, in almost every case. Non-urgent hospital waits for iron infusion in Toronto have been described as running from several weeks to several months. People who are symptomatic, or who have been waiting since a referral months ago, often decide the wait is the greater cost. That is a personal calculation, and it is a reasonable one, but it is worth confirming your actual place in the queue before assuming the wait will be long.

### Is an iron infusion the same as a vitamin drip?

No. An iron infusion is a medical treatment for a diagnosed deficiency, prescribed after bloodwork and monitored accordingly. A wellness vitamin drip is elective and is generally not aimed at correcting a documented deficiency. They may happen in similar looking rooms, but the clinical reasoning behind them is not comparable.

### Do I need a referral for an iron infusion?

For the hospital route, generally yes. Private clinics often accept patients without a formal referral, though many still want your recent bloodwork, and a clinic that wants to see your labs before treating you is behaving appropriately.
