## The real science the marketing borrows

NAD+, nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide, is a coenzyme present in every living cell, essential to converting food into energy and a required substrate for enzymes involved in DNA repair and cellular stress responses, the sirtuins and PARPs you may have seen named. Levels decline with age in many tissues, and that decline is an active area of legitimate aging research.

Every sentence above is real science, and every sentence appears, lightly reworded, in clinic marketing. The distance between that science and a wellness drip is where the story falls apart.

## The three-step leap the menus make

Step one: NAD+ is vital and declines with age. True. Step two: therefore raising it should slow aging. A hypothesis, being actively studied, mostly in animals and small human trials of oral precursors. Step three: therefore a 500 dollar infusion will deliver longevity, DNA repair and mental clarity. This is the step with no clinical evidence behind it.

What the human research actually shows so far is modest: oral precursors like NMN and NR reliably raise blood NAD+ levels, and the clinical endpoints that matter, function, healthspan, cognition, have shown limited and inconsistent effects in trials to date. For infusions specifically, the published evidence base is thinner still. No regulator has approved NAD+ infusion as a treatment for aging or any related claim.

## What the community reports, and why it matters

There is one more honest data point, and it comes from patients rather than papers. In community discussions, NAD+ generates more discomfort reports than anything else on the IV menu: chest tightness, cramping and nausea during infusion are commonly described, which is why sessions run for hours at controlled drip rates, and a minority describe feeling genuinely unwell afterward. These are anecdotes, not trials, but when the marketed product is a feeling and the reported experience is frequently an unpleasant one, the anecdotes are relevant to a purchase decision.

Put together: the highest price on the menu, 250 to 1,000 dollars and up per session with multi-session courses recommended, the least established benefit, and the most uncomfortable delivery. That combination is why our [drip-by-drip verdicts](/blog/iv-drip-verdicts-evidence-2026) calls NAD+ the most oversold item in the category.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does NAD+ IV therapy actually reverse aging?

No evidence supports that claim. NAD+ biology is a legitimate and active research field, but the human trial evidence so far, mostly on oral precursors, shows raised blood levels with limited and inconsistent effects on outcomes that matter, and infusion-specific evidence is thinner still. No regulator has approved NAD+ therapy for aging.

### Why do NAD+ IV sessions take so many hours?

Because infusing NAD+ quickly is uncomfortable: chest tightness, cramping and nausea are commonly reported when the rate is too fast, so clinics run it slowly over two to four hours or more. The session length is a management strategy for side effects, not a sign of potency.

### Is NAD+ IV better than NMN or NR supplements?

The IV delivers the molecule directly and the supplements are precursors your body converts, and both raise blood NAD+ levels. Given that the meaningful clinical benefits remain unestablished for both routes, the practical difference is that one costs cents per day and the other costs hundreds per session. The burden of proof sits with the expensive one.

### How much does NAD+ IV therapy cost in Canada?

Typically 250 to 1,000 dollars or more per session depending on dose, with clinics commonly recommending an initial series of several sessions. Full Canadian pricing and dosing tiers are in our dedicated NAD+ guide.

### Who should avoid NAD+ IV therapy?

Anyone for whom infusions generally carry added risk, kidney disease, heart failure, pregnancy, and anyone taking medications that have not been reviewed with a clinician, since screening practices vary widely between clinics. The general list is in our who-should-not guide, and a clinic that screens you carefully before a demanding infusion like NAD+ is showing you something important about itself.
