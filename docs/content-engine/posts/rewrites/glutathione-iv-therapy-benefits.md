Glutathione has the best origin story on the IV menu. It really is a molecule your cells make and use constantly, it really is central to how the body handles oxidative stress, and calling it the master antioxidant is not even marketing invention, the phrase appears in scientific literature. All of that is true, and none of it answers the question you actually have, which is whether paying 80 to 200 dollars to infuse it does anything for your skin, your liver, or your aging.

Here is the honest version. General information, not medical advice.

## What glutathione is

A small molecule made of three amino acids, produced by your own cells, involved in neutralizing reactive oxygen species and in the liver's processing of various compounds. Levels decline with age and are lower in some illnesses. Everything to this point is textbook biochemistry.

The marketing move is the next step: your levels decline, therefore topping them up by IV must help. Biology is rarely that obliging, and for glutathione the clinical evidence for the marketed benefits, brighter skin, detoxification, slowed aging, has not caught up with the theory. Small studies exist in both directions; what does not exist is the kind of consistent clinical evidence that would justify the confidence of the menus.

## The skin-brightening claim needs its own section

Glutathione is heavily marketed for skin brightening and, more bluntly in some markets, skin whitening. This is the one claim in the category with an actual regulatory record, and it is worth knowing.

The [US FDA has warned consumers directly](https://cacmap.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/injectable-skin-lightening-and-skin-bleaching-products-may-be-unsafe) that no injectable drug is approved for skin whitening or lightening in its jurisdiction, that such products are unapproved drugs, and that their risks include harm to the liver, kidneys and nervous system. Regulators elsewhere have issued similar warnings.

Canada's record is about the product itself. In late 2020, [Fraser Health alerted the public](https://www.fraserhealth.ca/employees/medical-health-officer-updates/alert-endotoxic-reactions-following-glutathione-injections-given-in-naturopath-offices) to endotoxic reactions, fever, vomiting, dangerously low blood pressure, after glutathione injections in BC naturopath offices. The trail led to a Burnaby supplier, and in early 2021 Health Canada [ordered a mandatory recall](https://recalls-rappels.canada.ca/en/alert-recall/unauthorized-injectables-and-other-health-products-galena-pharm-inc-pharmacy-may-pose) of more than a hundred unauthorized injectable products, including glutathione confirmed to be contaminated with endotoxins, that had been supplied to clinics in BC, Alberta and Ontario.

The lesson is not that every vial is dangerous. It is that with compounded injectables, where the product comes from matters more than what the poster in the lobby promises, and asking a clinic where its glutathione is compounded is the single most useful question you can ask before this particular drip.

## What a session involves and costs

Glutathione is usually given as a slow push or an add-on to another drip, taking a few minutes to half an hour, at 80 to 200 dollars per session on published Canadian menus. Clinics commonly recommend a series, which multiplies the math: a course of eight to ten sessions at those prices is a four-figure commitment for effects that, where they occur at all, published sources describe as gradual and temporary.

## An honest way to decide

- If your goal is the marketed skin effect, know that no regulator has approved injectable glutathione for it, and the strongest documented events in Canada in this category were contamination harms. That is the risk-reward picture the menu omits.
- If your goal is antioxidant support in general, your body makes its own glutathione from protein you eat, and sulfur-rich foods plus the boring fundamentals support that system for free.
- If you choose to book anyway, ask where the product is compounded, ask [who prescribes and administers](/blog/how-to-verify-iv-provider-license-canada-2026), disclose your medications, and check our guide to [who should not get IV therapy](/blog/who-should-not-get-iv-therapy-canada-2026) first.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does glutathione IV whiten or brighten skin?

Some people report temporary lightening effects, and the mechanism is biologically plausible, but no injectable drug is approved for skin whitening by the US FDA, which warns that such products are unapproved and potentially harmful. Where effects occur they are described as gradual and reversible, which means the marketed result requires indefinite paid sessions.

### Is glutathione IV safe?

The molecule itself is one your body makes, and reactions to properly made product are usually minor. The documented serious harms in Canada came from contaminated, unauthorized product supplied to clinics, which is why the provenance question, where is this compounded, matters more for glutathione than for almost anything else on the menu.

### Why not just take glutathione pills?

Oral glutathione is poorly absorbed intact, which is the true fact the IV marketing is built on. But your body synthesizes its own glutathione from dietary protein, so supporting the system through food is the default that costs nothing. The IV delivers the molecule directly; whether that produces the marketed benefits is the part the evidence has not established.

### How many sessions would I need?

Clinics typically recommend a series, often weekly for several weeks, then maintenance. At 80 to 200 dollars per session, that structure is worth seeing clearly: any effect is temporary, so the recommendation is effectively a subscription. Deciding whether to start is also deciding whether to continue indefinitely.
