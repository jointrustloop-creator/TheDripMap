Immune drips are sold on a simple picture: your immune system is a tank, vitamins are the fuel, and a drip fills it faster than food or pills. It is an appealing picture. It is not how immunity works, and the difference is worth understanding before you spend 150 to 300 dollars on a bag of vitamin C.

This guide covers what immune IV drips contain, what the evidence does and does not support, and the questions worth asking before you book one. General information, not medical advice.

## What is actually in an immune drip

The recipe varies by clinic, but the core is consistent: vitamin C at gram doses, zinc, B vitamins, and sometimes glutathione, in a base of saline. Each ingredient has a real role in immune function, which is precisely what makes the marketing plausible. The question is never whether vitamin C matters to immunity. It is whether pushing your blood levels above normal does anything for a person who was not deficient to begin with.

## What the evidence supports, and what it does not

Nutrient deficiency genuinely impairs immunity, and correcting a real deficiency genuinely helps. That much is solid. The leap the marketing makes is from there to the idea that surplus equals strength, and that leap is where the evidence stops.

For a well-nourished person, the body regulates blood vitamin levels tightly and excretes what it cannot use, a mechanism covered with the actual NIH figures in our [IV versus oral guide](/blog/iv-vs-oral-vitamins-absorption-canada-2026). Canada's health technology agency, reviewing the wellness infusion category, [found the evidence base thin](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK603628/). The [Cleveland Clinic's assessment](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/iv-vitamin-therapy) is the mainstream medical position in one line: people who are not deficient largely excrete the extra.

There is no published protocol by which scheduled vitamin infusions make a healthy person get sick less. A clinic offering a preventive protocol is offering a subscription, not a finding.

## The one safety note this category needs

High-dose vitamin C has one specifically documented risk: in people with G6PD deficiency, an inherited enzyme condition, gram doses have caused hemolysis, the destruction of red blood cells. In a published review of 14 cases, most affected patients did not know they had the condition beforehand. Asking a clinic what they do about G6PD before a high-dose vitamin C infusion is a fair, specific question, and the quality of the answer tells you a lot. The full picture is in our guide to [who should not get IV therapy](/blog/who-should-not-get-iv-therapy-canada-2026).

## What actually helps you get sick less

Unfashionable and free: adequate sleep, vaccination appropriate to you, hand hygiene through the cold season, managing stress, not smoking, and eating enough that you are not deficient in the first place. If you suspect you actually are deficient in something, that is a bloodwork conversation with your doctor, and if a deficiency turns up, the fix is usually oral and inexpensive.

None of this is as satisfying to buy as a drip. It is what the evidence supports.

## If you are considering one anyway

There are honest reasons people book immune drips: the ritual, the rest, feeling proactive during cold season, the placebo effect, which is real even when named. If that is worth the price to you, the checklist is the same as for any infusion:

- Ask for the ingredient list and doses, not just the drip's name.
- Disclose your medications and conditions, and expect to be asked.
- Ask about G6PD screening if the drip is high-dose vitamin C.
- [Verify who administers and who prescribes](/blog/how-to-verify-iv-provider-license-canada-2026) on a public register.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do immune IV drips actually prevent colds?

There is no good evidence that vitamin infusions make well-nourished people get sick less. Correcting a genuine deficiency helps immunity; adding surplus to a normal system mostly produces excretion. Neutral reviews of the category, including Canada's health technology agency, describe the evidence base as thin.

### Is high-dose vitamin C safe in an IV?

For most people the common effects are minor, but there is one well-documented risk: hemolysis in people with G6PD deficiency, most of whom did not know they had it before the reaction. A clinic offering gram-dose vitamin C should have a considered answer to what they do about G6PD. If they have never heard the question, that is an answer too.

### What is in an immune IV drip?

Typically vitamin C at gram doses, zinc, B vitamins and sometimes glutathione in saline. Recipes vary by clinic and are not standardized, which is why asking for the specific ingredient list and doses matters more than the name on the menu.

### Would a supplement do the same thing?

For nutrition, largely yes, at a small fraction of the price. Oral absorption at ordinary intakes is far better than the marketing implies, roughly 70 to 90 percent for vitamin C, and your body caps blood levels regardless of route. The honest advantages of the IV are speed and the experience, not better nutrition.
