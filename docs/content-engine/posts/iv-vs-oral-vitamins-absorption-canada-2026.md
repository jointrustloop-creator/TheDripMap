The most repeated sentence in IV therapy marketing is some version of this: pills only absorb a fraction, drips absorb 100 percent. It is the industry's favourite number, it appears on nearly every clinic menu, and it is doing a lot more selling than explaining.

Here is the thing: the 100 percent figure is technically true. An infusion goes straight into a vein, so nothing is lost to digestion. What the marketing leaves out is what your body does next, and whether the extra absorption buys you anything. This guide walks through what the actual absorption research says, where IV genuinely wins, and where it is an expensive way to make no difference. It is general information, not medical advice.

## What the absorption numbers actually are

Vitamin C is the best studied example, because researchers at the US National Institutes of Health mapped its pharmacokinetics in detail.

At ordinary intakes, oral absorption is not poor at all. According to the [NIH's professional fact sheet](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminC-HealthProfessional/), roughly 70 to 90 percent of vitamin C is absorbed at moderate intakes of 30 to 180 milligrams a day. The fraction only drops meaningfully at large doses: above a gram a day, absorption falls below 50 percent, with the rest passing through unabsorbed.

More importantly, your blood levels are actively managed. The foundational [pharmacokinetic work by Levine and colleagues](https://www.pnas.org/doi/pdf/10.1073/pnas.93.8.3704) found that plasma is essentially saturated at daily intakes around 1,000 milligrams, and that even aggressive oral dosing produces only modest further increases in blood concentration. Your kidneys excrete the surplus. This is the origin of the phrase you may have seen in coverage of the industry: beyond a point, you are producing expensive urine.

So the honest comparison is not 100 percent versus 20 percent. At the intakes that matter for nutrition, it is closer to 100 percent versus 70 to 90 percent, at perhaps a hundred times the price per session.

## What IV genuinely does differently

An infusion can push blood concentrations far above what any oral dose can reach, because it bypasses the gut's absorption ceiling entirely. That is a real physiological difference, and it is why intravenous vitamin C at very high doses is a subject of legitimate clinical research, mostly in oncology settings, under medical supervision, with screening.

The question for a healthy person buying a wellness drip is different: does briefly raising your blood level above the ceiling do anything for you? For general wellness claims, energy, immunity, glow, reviews by neutral institutions have not found convincing evidence that it does. The [Cleveland Clinic's assessment](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/iv-vitamin-therapy) is representative: people who are not deficient largely excrete the extra, and Canada's own health technology agency [found the evidence base thin](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK603628/) across the category. Higher blood levels are measurable. Benefits from them, in well-nourished people, have not been demonstrated.

## Where IV honestly wins

None of this means intravenous administration is a gimmick. There are situations where the gut is exactly the problem, and bypassing it is the point.

- **Malabsorption.** Conditions and surgeries that impair absorption can make oral supplementation genuinely ineffective. This is a medical situation with a medical answer.
- **B12 for pernicious anaemia.** A textbook case: the absorption mechanism itself is broken, so injection is the established route. It is specific enough that it is one of the few injectables named in Canada's [medical expense tax credit rules](/blog/iv-therapy-tax-deductible-canada-2026).
- **Diagnosed iron deficiency where oral iron fails.** Iron is the clearest example of a real infusion with real criteria, covered in our [iron infusion guide](/blog/iron-infusion-canada-cost-coverage-2026).
- **Clinical rehydration.** Someone who cannot keep fluids down is the original use case for IV fluids.

Notice the pattern: every honest win starts with a diagnosis, not a menu. If a clinic's reasoning begins with your bloodwork, you are in the left column. If it begins with a brand name and a price, you are in the right one.

## The cost math nobody puts on the menu

A standard vitamin drip in a Canadian city typically runs somewhere between 150 and 350 dollars a session, based on the published menus we track. The oral equivalents of its headline ingredients, vitamin C, B vitamins, magnesium, cost cents per day at the doses your body can actually use. Even granting IV its perfect absorption, you are paying a very large premium to route nutrients around a digestive system that, for most people, was absorbing them adequately for free.

That premium can still be worth it to someone who values the experience, the hour of rest, or the immediate feeling of a litre of fluids. Those are honest reasons. Absorption percentages are mostly not.

## Questions that cut through the pitch

- Am I actually deficient in anything? Has anyone checked?
- Would an oral supplement at a fraction of the price do the same thing for me?
- What specifically is in this bag, and at what doses?
- Who decided this formulation was right for me, and are they a prescriber I can [look up](/blog/how-to-verify-iv-provider-license-canada-2026)?

A clinic that engages with those questions honestly is worth more than any absorption statistic. ## Frequently asked questions

### Do IV vitamins absorb better than pills?

An IV bypasses digestion, so absorption is complete by definition, and it can raise blood levels higher than any oral dose. But at ordinary nutritional intakes, oral absorption is already around 70 to 90 percent for vitamin C, and the body actively excretes blood levels above its saturation point. Complete absorption of more than you can use is not better nutrition, it is faster excretion.

### Is the 100 percent absorption claim false?

No, and that is what makes it effective marketing. It is a true statement that implies a false comparison. The relevant question is not what fraction is absorbed but whether the extra absorption changes anything for you, and for well-nourished people, neutral reviews have not found evidence that it does.

### When is IV genuinely better than oral?

When the gut is the problem: malabsorption conditions, B12 deficiency from pernicious anaemia, diagnosed iron deficiency that oral iron cannot fix, and clinical rehydration when someone cannot keep fluids down. Each starts with a diagnosis. A wellness menu is not a diagnosis.

### Why do I feel better after an IV drip then?

Several honest possibilities: a litre of fluids when you were mildly dehydrated, an hour of enforced rest, expectation effects, or all three at once. Feeling better is real even when the mechanism is not the vitamins. The question is whether that feeling is worth the session price to you, knowing what is and is not established.

### How much does a vitamin drip cost compared to supplements?

Standard drips on Canadian published menus typically run 150 to 350 dollars a session, while oral versions of the same headline vitamins cost cents per day at usable doses. If a clinic argues the premium is justified by absorption, the figures above are the context that claim is missing.
