## Is IV vitamin therapy worth it?

For most healthy people, no strong evidence shows IV vitamin drips outperform food, water, and oral supplements. IV makes clear sense in specific cases: diagnosed deficiency, inability to absorb nutrients or keep fluids down, and clinician supervised treatment. For general wellness, you are mostly buying convenience and ritual.

That is the short version. The rest of this page is the reasoning, with sources, so you can check it yourself.

## What the evidence actually says

The research base for wellness drips is remarkably thin for an industry this size.

The only randomized, double blind, placebo controlled trial of the Myers cocktail tested it in 34 fibromyalgia patients and [found no significant difference versus a saline placebo](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19250003/) on the primary outcomes. Both groups improved, which tells you something important: feeling better after an infusion does not prove the vitamins did it.

There are no large trials showing that vitamin drips improve energy, immunity, skin, or recovery in healthy adults. When the United States Federal Trade Commission examined the claims of one IV bar chain, it brought [its first ever enforcement action against an IV therapy company](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2018/09/ftc-brings-first-ever-action-targeting-iv-therapy-company) for unsupported health claims. The clinics did not produce the evidence, because it does not exist.

None of this means every clinic is dishonest. It means the honest ones describe ingredients and leave the outcome promises off the menu, which is exactly what we score clinics on.

## The absorption argument, examined

The industry's favourite line is "100 percent bioavailability: IV bypasses digestion so you absorb everything." This is technically true and mostly beside the point.

A working digestive system absorbs what your body needs from food and oral supplements. For water soluble vitamins like C and the B family, your kidneys excrete the excess, whether it arrived by mouth or by vein. Pushing more in faster does not create a higher steady state; it creates expensive urine sooner.

Bypassing the gut matters when the gut is the problem: malabsorption conditions, some post surgical states, severe nausea and vomiting, or a diagnosed deficiency that oral dosing has failed to correct. Notice that every one of those is a medical situation with a clinician involved, not a wellness situation with a menu.

## When IV genuinely makes sense

- **Diagnosed iron deficiency.** Prescribed iron infusions treat a real, measured problem when oral iron fails or is not tolerated. This is medicine, not wellness, and it is the reason our [iron infusion guide](/blog/iron-infusion-canada-cost-coverage-2026) exists.
- **You cannot keep fluids down.** Vomiting from illness or severe morning sickness changes the calculus entirely, under medical care.
- **Clinician managed protocols.** Some physicians and naturopathic doctors use IV protocols for specific patients under ongoing care. Whether that is right for you is their conversation to have with you, with your history in front of them.

If you are in one of these situations, the question is not "is IV worth it" but "is this clinic competent," and for that we built the [verification guide](/verification).

## IV drip vs drinking water: which hydrates you better?

For mild dehydration, drinking water or an oral rehydration solution works for nearly everyone, and it is what clinical guidance recommends first. An IV rehydrates faster and is the right tool when someone cannot drink or keep fluids down, under clinical supervision.

Oral rehydration is the global standard of care for mild to moderate dehydration; the [World Health Organization's oral rehydration protocols](https://www.who.int/teams/health-product-policy-and-standards/standards-and-specifications/norms-and-standards-for-pharmaceuticals/oral-rehydration-salts) exist because drinking the right fluids works, cheaply, almost everywhere. An IV line delivers fluid faster, which matters in emergencies and when the mouth is not an option. For a person who feels rough after a workout or a night out, a glass of water and time do the same job as a $200 bag, minus the needle.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do IV vitamins work better than oral vitamins?

For people with a normally functioning gut, oral vitamins reach the levels your body will actually use, and the excess of water soluble vitamins is excreted either way. IV delivery matters when absorption is medically impaired or a clinician is correcting a diagnosed deficiency.

### Are IV drips a scam?

The infusions are real and the ingredients are real. The gap is between what is in the bag and what the marketing implies it will do. Regulators have acted against unsupported claims, and no strong trial evidence supports wellness benefits in healthy adults. Whether that makes it a scam or an expensive comfort service depends on how it is sold to you.

### Is IV therapy worth it for a hangover?

The evidence is thin, because a hangover is not mainly a dehydration problem. Fluids help the dehydration component; time does the rest. Our [drip by drip evidence guide](/blog/iv-drip-verdicts-evidence-2026) covers what each popular ingredient does and does not have behind it.

### When should I choose an IV over drinking water?

When you medically cannot drink or keep fluids down, when a clinician has diagnosed a deficiency that oral treatment has not fixed, or in emergency settings. Outside those cases, oral hydration is the first line answer in clinical guidance.
